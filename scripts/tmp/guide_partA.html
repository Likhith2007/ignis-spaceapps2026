01 · The mission
Why IGNIS exists

NASA's two flagship fire instruments are split across two eras and two scales. MODIS (1 km resolution) has flown on Terra since 2000 and Aqua since 2002; VIIRS (375 m) began on S-NPP in 2012 and continues on NOAA-20 (2018) and NOAA-21 (2023). A fire small enough for VIIRS to see can be invisible to MODIS — so the same landscape produces wildly different detection counts depending on which satellite you ask. That split record breaks every long-term analysis: 26 years of MODIS cannot be compared to 14 years of VIIRS without harmonization.

IGNIS answers the challenge statement — Harmonization of MODIS and VIIRS Hot Spots — with a single, continuous, calibrated Burning Activity Calendar from 2000 to today, then layers everything an early-warning user actually needs on top: boundary-identified fire clusters, persistent-system tracking, daily dynamics, fire-weather fusion, impact accounting, personal alerting, and a grounded AI analyst that writes incident-style situation reports. Every number in the product is computed from the live record or a labeled, versioned dataset — nothing is decorative, nothing is invented.

Goal 1 — HarmonizationGoal 2 — Web applicationColorblind-safe rampsDownloadable products

The official brief asks for three concrete deliverables: a harmonized record (Goal 1), a web application where a user picks a region and date range and receives the fire history, trend and abnormality calendar (Goal 2), and downloadable products with accessible color ramps. Section 12 maps every IGNIS feature to those deliverables line by line.

02 · The console
Mission-control layout & regions

IGNIS is a single-page mission control modeled on the consoles fire analysts actually use — NASA Worldview, Zoom Earth, Windy and nullschool. One map, one summary line, one rail of live indicators, and a deep analytics workspace behind it. The design contract is simple: the map is the hero. Every overlay collapses to a compact chip by default and expands on demand, the map can go fullscreen without touching the page grid, and every panel is one click away.

Regions & the AOI selector

The selector in the header defines where every panel looks. Nine curated regions are chosen for fire-signal diversity — Amazon, Congo, Siberia, Boreal North America, California, Mediterranean, Australia, Southeast Asia — plus a Bangladesh spotlight, the team's home region, with six sub-AOIs that surface the Boro rice-residue burning season (March–April) exactly as it emerges from the real NASA record. Two mechanisms make the whole planet available: search any place on Earth (an OSM Nominatim proxy geocoder flies the map there and refetches every panel) and ⌖ draw a custom box directly on the map. The drawn box is a first-class AOI: the 26-year calendar, the science lab and the downloads all recalculate for it, which is precisely the "region + date → fire history" flow the brief asks for. A Wikipedia-powered RegionContext placard quietly answers "what am I looking at?" for whatever region is active.

TopBrief — the five-second answer

One computed sentence sits at the top of the console — detections in the last 24 hours, total fire radiative power, the MODIS/VIIRS split — plus an EARLY ALERT banner that escalates GREEN → AMBER → ORANGE → RED from live FRP and density. The summary is produced by a deterministic rule engine, never an LLM, so it cannot hallucinate and never blocks: if every AI endpoint on Earth went dark, the brief would still be perfect. The alert strip only appears at ORANGE/RED, auto-dismisses, and carries a breathing severity beacon.

03 · The map
The living fire map

The map is IGNIS's heart: a NASA GIBS WMTS basemap with the live fire record drawn on top. It runs on two independent renderers chosen by a WebGL2 capability probe that executes before the GL map is ever constructed. Modern browsers get MapLibre GL with GPU acceleration; browsers with blocked GPUs (Brave strict shields, VMs, headless CI runners) get the Leaflet fallback with zero WebGL — a full interactive map instead of the GPUInitializationError crash that originally motivated the design. The escape hatch ?nogpu=1 forces the fallback for support and automated screenshots.

The BLAZE fire-dot engine · v8

The Leaflet renderer replaced ~75k per-point markers with one custom canvas layer: a pre-rendered radial-gradient sprite cache (glow / core / night / white-hot) composited with an additive "lighter" glow pass for real fire optics, an FRP-ascending paint order so the hottest fires draw last and stay visible, DPR-crisp retina rendering, and viewport culling with a 30,000-detection cap.

Colors follow the proper-red ramp: every detection reads as a red fire dot at any zoom — pale red for small burns deepening to crimson with FRP — instead of an old yellow→red ramp in which small fires washed out against bright basemaps. Size scales with √FRP; detections above 150 MW get white-hot cores; night-mode sprites key off acquisition time.

Every dot is interactive · spatial index

A uniform-grid spatial index (0.05° cells) makes every hotspot hoverable and clickable at any zoom — hover shows an rAF-throttled tooltip; click opens the full AI event card: threat badge (LOW → EXTREME), fire-type classification, a 6-hour spread forecast upgraded by live wind, smoke & air context, a CO₂e estimate, and a one-tap handoff that carries the card's telemetry into the AI Analyst. Pulses, ember drift, fire-front blooms and spread rings animate over active clusters; an additive heatmap mode renders the same detections as a density surface; a hexbin layer aggregates them into hexagonal bins.

Color modes & layers

  • Four style modes recolor every detection: intensity (FRP ramp), sensor (MODIS vs VIIRS hues — the harmonization story at a glance), confidence (low/nominal/high) and heatmap (density surface). The GL path adds a fires-ember underglow (FRP ≥ 45 MW) so mid-size fires read as live embers.
  • NASA GIBS basemaps, date-synced to the selected day: true color, 7-2-1 false color (burn scars read magenta) and Blue Marble.
  • MOD11 LST land-surface-temperature overlay using the official LP DAAC V6 decode (LST = stored × 0.02 K, QA bits decoded right→left) with the official hot-red / cold-blue palette.
  • GIBS fire-pixel vector tiles — an independent NASA pipeline cross-checking FIRMS on the same night.
  • Wind streamlines (the nullschool signature, on by default) drawn live from Open-Meteo and dimming automatically while an AI event card is open; RainViewer radar and cloud frames fetched on demand so the map stays clean until asked.
  • Windy-style master opacity slider with per-layer base opacities, plus a 🔮 next-day risk field overlay from the footprint-forecaster model (section 10).

Time travel & focus

A 24H / 48H / 7D window switcher and a 7-day replay step the record day by day with persistent fire-system tracking (nearest-centroid matching + FRP trend): you can watch a complex ignite, merge and burn out across the week. ⛶ Focus mode locks page scroll, resizes the renderer to the full viewport, re-measures on every geometry change, and exits with Esc — a true fullscreen map without leaving the page.

04 · The third dimension
3D modes — 🧊 Fire Field & ⛰ Terrain towers

Two WebGL-first 3D views share the map's date, AOI and sensor filters, and each carries a GPU-free fallback so 3D never hard-fails. 🧊 Fire Field extrudes detections as a volumetric fire field over a dark grid, with height and brightness following FRP; where WebGL is unavailable it degrades to an isometric canvas fire field carrying the same information. ⛰ Terrain towers renders detections as 3D towers over a Three.js terrain scene, and the GL path adds a terrain/extrusion feel to the main map camera. A live threat rollup in the HUD stays visible while you orbit. The point is legibility: judges and responders alike read which complex carries the energy faster in 3D than in any 2D color ramp.
